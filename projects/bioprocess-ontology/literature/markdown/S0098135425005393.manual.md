# On the impact of mechanistic model quality and data availability in hybrid model development

PII: `S0098135425005393`
DOI: `10.1016/j.compchemeng.2025.109536`

## Abstract

This work presents a methodology for evaluating the effectiveness of hybrid modelling under varying conditions of mechanistic model quality and information available for model training, that is typically expressed as the amount of data available. While hybrid models – which integrate mechanistic and data-driven components – have gained significant attention in process systems engineering, their advantages over purely mechanistic or data-driven alternatives remain inadequately quantified. We address this gap by investigating two critical factors: (i) the impact of mechanistic model fidelity on hybrid model performance, and (ii) the influence of calibration dataset size on prediction accuracy. Our methodology is validated through an in-silico case study of baker's yeast cultivation and a real-world industrial application of ion-exchange chromatography in biopharmaceutical manufacturing. Results demonstrate that hybrid models consistently outperform purely mechanistic and data-driven approaches when the mechanistic component captures fundamental process behaviours, even with structural simplifications. Notably, hybrid models maintain superior predictive capability in extrapolative scenarios; however, when mechanistic knowledge is severely limited and insufficient information is available for compensation, hybridisation benefits diminish substantially. The work provides quantitative guidance for practitioners to determine when hybrid modelling represents a justified investment of modelling resources in process engineering applications.

## Introduction

In recent years, a variety of modelling approaches have gained significant attention to support the design and optimisation of engineering processes across diverse industrial sectors ( Zhang et al., 2019 ). The growing interest in adopting mathematical models is motivated by the increasing demand for more efficient and reliable approaches to process development and decision-making, especially in contexts where experimental campaigns are costly, time-consuming, or limited by operational constraints ( Wang et al., 2024 ). The level of knowledge and understanding of the process, as well as the amount of information required for calibration and validation of a model at a given stage of process development, can vary significantly, affecting the selection of the modelling method ( Chen and Ierapetritou, 2020 ; Zendehboudi et al., 2018 ).

Depending on the extent of the underlying physics that is captured by the model equations, mathematical models are generally categorized into three main types ( Bonvin et al., 2016 ; Moser et al., 2020 ): mechanistic models (also referred to as knowledge-driven, first-principles, or white-box models), data-driven models (often called empirical or black-box models), and hybrid models (also known as semi-empirical or grey box models). One of the primary benefits of mechanistic models is their ability to enhance understanding of products and processes by explicitly describing the physical, chemical, and biological properties of the system under investigation. They typically require few calibration experiments and provide superior extrapolation capabilities compared to data-driven approaches. However, deriving their equations may be complex, as it involves identifying the exact underlying physical phenomena that characterize the process under analysis, and demands more time, resources, and modelling effort, often requiring high computational burden ( Solle et al., 2017 ). In cases of limited knowledge and process understanding, a mechanistic modelling approach may be insufficient to reliably enable simulation-based development and optimisation, often resulting in process-model mismatch, i.e., a discrepancy between predictions and experimental data.

On the other hand, empirical models only rely on available historical data. The main advantages include their ability to capture correlation structures within data with no need of detailed understanding of the underlying physics, rapid development timelines, and reduced computational demands, particularly when they offer simplified representations of highly complex mechanistic models ( Reis and Saraiva, 2019 ; Rendall and Reis, 2018 ). Nonetheless, they typically require extensive calibration datasets, which may be limited or inconsistent in terms of structure and quality. Furthermore, generalization and interpretability can be more challenging since their performance may strongly reduce when applied to explorative scenarios ( Sansana et al., 2021 ).

When tackling real-world problems, all available sources of information (i.e., physical understanding of the system and historical process data) should be leveraged. In this context, hybrid modelling strategies are effective alternatives aiming to integrate the physical knowledge embedded in the available mechanistic model with empirical data ( von Stosch et al., 2014 ). As a result, hybrid configurations preserve core insight into the underlying physical process mechanisms while extracting information from available data-driven patterns that cannot be captured by first-principles knowledge, thereby enhancing both predictive accuracy and interpretability ( Sansana et al., 2021 ). Additionally, hybrid models can contribute to reducing the overall time required for model development. In general, extending a mechanistic model to include more detailed knowledge can be a time-consuming task ( Schweidtmann et al., 2024 ). By leveraging the flexibility of data-driven components, part of the knowledge not explicitly captured in the mechanistic equations can instead be extracted by appropriately modelling the available process data, thereby accelerating the construction of reliable process models.

Over the last years, various studies have explored the potential of hybrid models across different domains. For instance, Schäfer et al. (2019) used artificial neural networks combined with differential equations to model rectification columns, and the model was later used for real-time applications ( Lüthje et al., 2021 ). Bui et al. (2021) proposed a hybrid model configuration combining a fundamental adiabatic reactor model with an empirical partial least-squares regression to monitor and predict the catalyst lifetime of an operating industrial reactor. Shah et al. (2022) demonstrated the high predictive capability of a deep neural network-based hybrid model for an industry-scale fermentation process, outperforming a purely mechanistic kinetic model. Polak et al. (2023) developed a hybrid modelling approach to evaluate the effects of raw material variations and process parameter fluctuations on product quantity and quality in a flow chemistry process for small molecules manufacturing. Xie et al. (2024) employed a neural network to compensate for the unmodeled dynamics of the first-principles model on an industrial nitration process under load-changing scenarios. Mahanty et al. (2025) proposed a hybrid model to predict diesel fuel removal dynamics, with the instantaneous removal rate modelled using an artificial neural network. Michalopoulou and Papathanasiou (2025) integrated artificial neural networks with process knowledge to describe the nonlinear dynamics of a chromatographic separation process. Caspari et al. (2025) provided evidence that hybrid models remain effective in data-scarce scenarios, highlighting their robustness with limited information. Steinacker et al. (2025) explored the potential of hybrid models based on different neural networks and compared the performance to typically used mechanistic models to predict atypical trajectories of hematotoxicity damage to chemotherapy patients. Wang et al. (2025) demonstrated the improved performances of physics-data-driven hybrid models to predict accurately the friction torque in hydraulic motors.

However, not only do hybrid models require a careful integration of system knowledge and historical data, but they also necessitate a deep understanding of when and how each component of the model (i.e., the mechanistic and data-driven ones) can contribute to the overall hybrid performance ( Rogers et al., 2023 ; Bradley et al., 2022 ). Developing a comprehensive framework to systematically construct and integrate these components remains challenging ( Shah et al., 2024 ). This represents a clear need for further development to ensure that hybrid approaches can be more systematically and effectively applied in process engineering ( Schweidtmann et al., 2024 ). Most published contributions analyzing how the quality each component or the limited amount of information available for model training impact on the overall hybrid model performance are based on synthetic or simulated datasets, with a notable lack of studies addressing real-world processes in a generalizable framework. These works are also generally limited to specific architectures ( Narayanan et al., 2022 ).

Our methodology is designed for cases in which models are developed for offline analysis and optimization purposes, such as for process design, scale-up and simulation. While several studies have explored hybrid modelling architectures and performance metrics, most contributions do not quantify the relative impact of mechanistic accuracy and data availability on the predictive reliability of hybrid models. This work specifically addresses this gap by analysing how these two factors interact and affect model robustness under both in-silico and industrial conditions.

Our investigation encompasses both in-silico and real-world case studies. Specifically, an in-silico study conducted on a fed-batch reactor system for baker’s yeast production is used to address key challenges, such as the limited predicative capability of available mechanistic models and the paucity of data, and questions the effectiveness of hybrid modelling under such constraints. An additional in-silico case study concerning urethane synthesis is presented in the Supplementary Material. Finally, a real-world case study concerning ion-exchange chromatography in biopharmaceutical manufacturing aims at providing insights concerning typical challenges in industrial applications.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 , we describe the general methodology for the analysis and construction of suitable hybrid modelling configurations. In Section 3 , we give details about the in-silico and real-world case studies, respectively. Results are presented and critically discussed in Section 4 . Final remarks conclude the manuscript.

## Methodology

### General methodology

The methodology is structured into a sequence of steps, each of them contributing to the systematic construction and evaluation of suitable hybrid models ( Fig. 1 ). Here, we aim to assess: ( i ) the impact of different levels of complexity in the mechanistic component, and ( ii ) the effect of the amount of information available on the overall hybrid model performance. These aspects are crucial to evaluate the robustness and generalization capabilities of the methodology under various practical scenarios. The methodological steps are outlined in Fig. 1 .

#### Step #1 – mechanistic model calibration and validation

We start from an available mechanistic model, which is assumed to provide an approximate representation of the system under investigation, based on the modeler's prior knowledge. This validation strategy allows to assess both model accuracy under familiar conditions, but also its generalization capabilities when applied to unseen regions of the experimental domain.

#### Step #2 – construction of hybrid model

While mechanistic models offer a structured and interpretable description of the underlying physical and chemical phenomena, they often suffer from structural inaccuracies due to simplifications, unmodeled dynamics, or missing mechanisms. To account for process-model mismatch, a hybrid modelling structure that integrates a data-driven component into the mechanistic framework can be used. Specifically, we propose an architecture where the mechanistic component provides initial predictions of the states and outputs of the system ( Sansana et al., 2021 ); The data-driven component takes the mechanistic component outputs, along with the experimental inputs available, and is constructed to model the estimation error of the mechanistic component, thus compensating for the observed mismatch ( Fig. 2 ).

The proposed architecture differs from the typical serial or parallel configurations reported in the literature ( Schäfer et al., 2020 ; Shah et al., 2024 ). It combines elements of both approaches: similar to parallel structures, the data-driven component corrects the output of the mechanistic model by compensating for its systematic deviations. At the same time, it shares a feature with serial structures, since the data-driven model leverages the information provided by the mechanistic model itself. This results in a formulation that benefits from both strategies, without being a conventional instance of either.

Random forest (RF) models are employed as data-driven components ( Breiman, 2001 ). They make output predictions by combining the outcomes from a sequence of regression decision trees, called forest; each tree depends on the values of a random vector sampled independently and with the same distribution for all trees in the forest. The output value for the RF is simply the average value of the outputs for every decision tree in the forest.

The selection of RFs as data-driven component is motivated by their ability to capture nonlinear relationships, and because they tend to perform well in low-data regimes while exhibiting relatively low risk of overfitting ( He and Garcia, 2009 ). However, it should be noted that the signal predicted by RFs may appear noisy, since the implementation does not account for temporal autocorrelation. As a result, the predictions may lack smoothness, and a filtering step was applied to dampen these fluctuations ( Blackledget, 2006 ). The moving average y ˜ i of a series y i over a window size ω is defined as: (3) y ˜ i = 1 ω ∑ j = − k k y i + j , where ω = 2 k +1 is the window size, and k is the number of data points on either side of y i . The window size ω should be large enough to smooth high-frequency noise, while at the same time retain the significant features of the signal, i.e., the predicted output.

The key hyperparameter of a RF is the number of trees in the forest; in this work, the optimal value is determined by minimizing the root-mean squared error through cross-validation ( He and Garcia, 2009 ). Alternative optimisation strategies are also possible, such as a grid-search approach ( Bergstra et al., 2013 ).

RFs demonstrated very good performance in the case studies considered in this work. Nonetheless, other machine learning algorithms – such as artificial neural networks ( Haykin, 2009 ), Gaussian processes ( Rasmussen and Williams, 2005 ), partial least square (PLS) regression models ( Geladi and Kowalski, 1996 ) or support vector machines ( Drucker et al., 2002 ) – may also be considered, depending on the characteristics of the system and the availability of data.

In the construction of the hybrid model configuration, the data-driven component is the only one subject to parameter estimation (i.e., the decision rules at each tree node). The mechanistic model parameters, which have been calibrated beforehand (see Section 2.1.1 ), are kept fixed. This defines our sequential approach, where the mechanistic component is first calibrated independently, and subsequently used as the static backbone of the hybrid configuration. While this sequential approach may not guarantee global optimality in terms of overall model performance ( Schweidtmann et al., 2024 ), it is deliberately adopted here for two reasons: ( i ) as the mechanistic component is assumed to represent the best available knowledge, it serves as fixed backbone of the hybrid model structure, and ( ii ) avoiding re-calibration of the mechanistic part allows for a clean and interpretable comparison across different scenarios, isolating the contribution and compensatory effect of the data-driven component without the influence of re-estimating the mechanistic parameters.

A simultaneous approach may lead to global optimality in terms of prediction accuracy. However, such an approach was not considered since it also introduces additional complexity: (i) a risk of losing parameter interpretability, as the optimization may compensate mechanistic inaccuracies through parameter values with no physical meaning; (ii) higher computational cost due to the enlarged parameter space.

#### Step #3 – evaluation of model performance

The final step focuses on evaluating whether the integration of the data-driven component into the mechanistic component results in a measurable improvement over purely mechanistic or purely data-driven models, and, if so, to what extent this improvement is achieved. To this end, the hybrid model performance should be compared against that of the standalone mechanistic and empirical models. The quality of fitting is measured using the mean absolute error ( M A E ) : (4) M A E = 1 N s p ∑ i = 1 N s p | y i − y ^ i | .

### Computational details

All activities were performed on an Intel Core I7- 11850H CPU@2.50 GHz processor with 64.0 GB RAM or an Intel Core I9 - 13900H@2.60 GHz processor with 64 GB RAM. All models were implemented as custom Python scripts (version 3.12).

The generation of artificial datasets for the in-silico case studies required only a few minutes per case. In contrast, parameter estimation for mechanistic models proved to be more computationally intensive, ranging from tens of minutes (e.g., baker’s yeast growth and chromatography studies) to several hours (for the case study concerning urethane production process presented in the Supplementary Material ), depending on model complexity and the amount of experimental data used for calibration. Parameter estimation of hybrid and purely data-driven models typically required between a few minutes and several tens of minutes, depending on the size of the calibration dataset.

## Case studies

Two case studies will be presented in the following sections. The first case study involves a simulated fed-batch bioreactor and serves as an in-silico testbed for exploring how hybrid model performance is affected by mechanistic component simplification and amount of information available.

The second case study focuses on a real-world ion-exchange chromatography process, aiming to test the applicability and robustness of the proposed methodology in the presence of complex physicochemical mechanisms and industrial data constraints.

An additional in-silico case study of increasing complexity is discussed in the Supplementary Material.

### Baker’s yeast growth

#### Process

The first process is yeast cultivation in a fed-batch bioreactor. We aim at building a model that predicts the dynamics of yeast and substrate concentrations. This is an in-silico case study; the process is assumed to be perfectly described by the following set of mass balance equations ( Aiba et al., 1968 ): (5) d y 1 d t = ( r − u 1 − θ 4 ) y 1 , (6) d y 2 d t = − r y 1 θ 3 + u 1 ( u 2 − y 2 ) , (7) r = θ 1 y 2 θ 2 + y 2 e − y 2 θ 5 , where y 1 and y 2 are (respectively) the biomass and substrate concentrations inside the reactor, u 1 is the dilution factor, u 2 is the substrate concentration in the feed. The number of parameters is 5, and they are assumed to be known and fixed at their nominal values ( Table 1 ).

Datasets are generated by solving the model Eqs. (5) - (7) with parameter values as in Table 1 . A variety of experimental conditions is considered by varying manipulated variables and initial conditions across different batch runs, with values randomly drawn from predefined intervals ( Table 2 ). Specifically, the control variables u 1 and u 2 are treated as piecewise-constant functions with a minimum step duration of 0.5 h, and can vary over 5 steps, while initial concentrations y 1 0 and y 2 0 are random integers. Each simulated batch has a duration of 10 h, with measurements recorded every 10 s. To emulate experimental uncertainty and measurement noise, Gaussian noise with variance σ 2 = 0.02 g L −1 is added to the output profiles of the measured variables y 1 and y 2 ( Galvanin et al., 2011 ). For the theoretical analysis, either all or a subset of the generated datasets are used, allowing the assessment of the impact of amount of available data on hybrid model performance.

#### Process

This case study comes from an industrial biopharmaceutical process. The experimental data concerns monoclonal antibody (mAb) load material with a concentration of 9.12 g L −1 , and an average content of heavy and light ends equal to 2.3 % and 1.1 %, respectively. The experimental setup consists in the ion-exchange column Capto SP ImpRes–HiScreen (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA), with bed height of 10 cm, and inner diameter of 0.77 cm. Column bead and interstitial column porosities are equal to 80.6 %, and 38.3 %, respectively; column axial dispersion coefficient, D a x , is 0.22 mm 2 s −1 . To perform the elution, either a linear gradient or constant salt concentration is used, with the first buffer containing 500 mM sodium acetate, and the second one not containing salts.

Outlet concentration values of mAb for five experimental runs are available (A, B, C, D, E), together with conductivity data and changes in modulator concentration along the elution time span; load velocity, u , is set at 0.22 mm s −1 for all runs.

The model accounts for convective transport, axial dispersion, and adsorption kinetics, and assumes negligible concentration gradients within the stationary phase. ## Results

### Baker’s yeast growth

#### Mechanistic model calibration and validation

To evaluate the effect of the available mechanistic component within the proposed hybrid modelling framework, we begin by calibrating the three mechanistic models that incorporate varying degrees of process knowledge, as described in Section 3.1 .

Calibration is performed under a large-data availability scenario, namely using 100 batches for parameter estimation and 10 batches for validation. In validation, we distinguish between in-domain prediction (V-I) – i.e., considering validation batches that follow input conditions and initial states that fall within the range used during calibration – and two types of extrapolations, i.e., ( i ) extrapolation on input trajectories (V-E1) – in which the time profiles of manipulated variables differ from those used during calibration – and ( ii ) extrapolation on initial conditions (V-E2) – in which the initial states lie outside the range observed during calibration. These scenarios are specifically designed to evaluate the generalisation capabilities of the models beyond the calibration domain. Table 3 summarizes the estimated parameters for each mechanistic model, and Fig. 3 compares their predictive performance using a representative example (batch #1) from the validation batches.

The predictive performance of the three mechanistic models is evaluated using the M A E ( Table 4 ). As expected, the best-performing mechanistic model (i.e., mechanistic model #1) shows consistently lower M A E values. In contrast, the other mechanistic models exhibit a marked decline in predictive accuracy in extrapolation, particularly for different initial conditions. Overall, while a high-fidelity mechanistic model provides reliable predictions even outside the calibration domain, simplifications in the model structure compromise its performance.

As a reference for subsequent comparison, we also construct a purely data-driven RF model on the same calibration datasets, which uses the process inputs u 1 and u 2 to directly predict the output variables, y 1 and y 2 . The purely data-driven model provides good accuracy for in-domain predictions (V-I), whose performance significantly worsens in extrapolation settings (V-E1 and V-E2) ( Table 4 ).

#### Construction of the hybrid model

We proceed with the construction of hybrid models as described in Section 2.1.2 . They are built on top of each of the three mechanistic components. For each hybrid model, RFs are obtained based on the same calibration datasets adopted in Section 4.1.1 . The data-driven inputs are the same process variables used in the mechanistic component (i.e., u 1 and u 2 ), together with the outputs predicted by the mechanistic component (i.e., y 1 and y 2 ) over time. RFs hyperparameters are optimised via five-fold cross-validation, with a selected number of 100 trees for all cases. The same validation settings as in Section 4.1.1 are leveraged to assess the benefits of the hybrid models (i.e., V-I, V-E1, and V-E2).

Quantitative analysis of the M A E values reported in Table 5 and percentages of improvement over the standalone mechanistic and data-driven models in Table 6 clearly highlights the advantages of hybridisation, especially over mechanistic models #2 and #3. For instance, in the most challenging extrapolation scenario (V-E2), the hybrid model built on mechanistic component #3 reduces the MAE for y 2 from 3.17 g L −1 (purely mechanistic) and 2.75 g L −1 (purely data-driven) down to 1.14 g L −1 , corresponding to a 64 % and 59 % reduction, respectively.

Similarly, in the in-domain setting (V-I), the hybrid model built on mechanistic component #2 lowers the M A E for y 1 by 87 % compared to the corresponding mechanistic model (from 1.05 to 0.14 g L −1 ), and by 50 % compared to the purely data-driven one (from 0.28 to 0.14 g L −1 ). These results confirm that even limited mechanistic knowledge, when suitably combined with flexible data-driven components, can lead to models with robust predictive capabilities across a broad range of scenarios.

#### Effect of the amount of information available

In this section, we analyse how the amount of calibration data impacts the predictive capability of the hybrid model. To this end, we repeat the procedure described in Sections 4.1.1 and 4.1.2 , using mechanistic model #1 and mechanistic model #3 as mechanistic components, which represent the two extremes in terms of predictive capabilities (i.e., the best and the worst performances, respectively). The analysis is conducted by reducing the available calibration dataset to 10 % ( Table 7 ) of its original size, and evaluating the resulting accuracy under the same validation scenarios.

Fig. 5 illustrates the predictive performance for the two hybrid models and the data-driven model, using the same validation batch as in Sections 4.1.1 and 4.1.2 , when calibration is performed with a decreasing-size dataset that was obtained through random selection. The mechanistic models are not included in the comparison in Fig. 5 , given that their performance does not significantly vary with the amount of calibration data.

Results show that a substantial reduction in data availability to only 10 % of the original dataset size does not lead to a notable decline in predictive performance. Compared to the purely data-driven model, both hybrid models consistently demonstrate superior robustness to reductions in calibration data, especially the one built upon mechanistic component #1, which embeds the higher level of mechanistic knowledge. The superior resilience of hybrid model built upon mechanistic component #1 proves the pivotal role played by the underlying mechanistic component in determining the overall performance of hybrid frameworks, particularly when experimental data are limited.

Key findings from the analysis of hybrid model performance under different levels of mechanistic knowledge and information available can be summarized as follows. • The accuracy of the mechanistic component strongly influences the overall hybrid model performance. While hybrid models consistently outperform purely data-driven models across all cases, the magnitude of improvement is smaller when the mechanistic component poorly represents the system (e.g., mechanistic model #3); • The effect of the dataset reduction (down to 10 % of the original dataset size) is more evident for the hybrid model that incorporates the poorly performing mechanistic component (i.e., mechanistic model #3), where the limited information contained in a small dataset cannot fully compensate for the inadequacy of the mechanistic model; • The advantages of hybrid models can be immediately appreciated under in-domain conditions (V-I). In this setting, all models, including purely mechanistic and data-driven models, show relatively good predictive accuracy. However, hybrid models still retain a consistent edge. For example, when leveraging the full calibration dataset (100 batches), the hybrid configuration based on mechanistic component #1 achieves an M A E of 0.06 g L⁻¹ for y 1 compared to 0.26 g L⁻¹ for the mechanistic model and 0.28 g L⁻¹ for the purely data-driven one; similarly, the M A E for y 2 is 0.21 g L⁻¹, versus 0.59 (mechanistic) and 1.36 (data-driven). • The benefits of hybrid models become more evident when the prediction task involves extrapolation, i.e., moving beyond the conditions considered for calibration. In the most challenging validation scenario (V-E2) and still considering case of full calibration dataset, the hybrid configuration built upon mechanistic component #1 (which embeds the most informative process description) shows very low M A E values, corresponding to improvements of 34 % and 74 % for y 1 , and 31 % and 72 % for y 2 , when compared to the purely mechanistic and data-driven models, respectively. Remarkably, even the hybrid model built upon mechanistic component #3 – despite relying on more limited process knowledge – delivers substantial gains. For y 1 , the MAE is reduced by 64 % relative to the mechanistic model and 66 % relative to the data-driven one. For y 2 , the corresponding reductions are 64 % and 59 %.

### Ion-exchange chromatography

#### Mechanistic model calibration and validation

Focusing on the main component, i.e., the mAb, mechanistic model parameters of both models are estimated using four of the five available experiments ( Section S.2 in the Supplementary Material ) – which represents a very low amount of data for model calibration. This procedure is repeated by alternatively excluding one experiment, ensuring that each one is used for validation once. The mean parameter values reported in Table 8 are computed as the arithmetic average over all estimation runs.

The accuracy of the two mechanistic models is quantitatively evaluated using the M A E and the normalized peak error ( P E ), which is defined as: (25) P E = | t ^ p e a k − t e x p , p e a k | Δ t , where t ^ p e a k and t e x p , p e a k are the time at which the maximum value of predicted output and the experimental counterpart (respectively) appear; Δ t is the time span in which the experimental output concentration is non-zero. This metric serves as an effective indicator of the accuracy in forecasting the time of peak concentration, which is a critical information for the specific application ( Guiochon et al., 2006 ).

Fig. 6 illustrates the predictive performance of the calibrated mechanistic models (parameters as in Table 8 ) across all available datasets, while accuracy metrics are reported in Table 9 . For both models, predictions are poor, suggesting that the available model structures cannot describe well the underlying physical phenomena. Notably, the LKM coupled with the simple Langmuir adsorption isotherm exhibits substantial limitations under conditions with varying salt concentrations in the mobile phase, which induce complex binding interactions, as seen in datasets A, C, and E. In these cases, the model fails to capture delayed elution peaks, as indicated by the high P E values. This is consistent with the fact that, conversely, datasets B and D are characterized by constant salt concentration and lack of peak distortion, which reduce nonlinear binding effects and render the system dynamics more consistent with the assumptions of the simple Langmuir adsorption isotherm.

In contrast, the ASMA isotherm demonstrates enhanced descriptive capabilities, particularly in capturing the temporal characteristics of elution peaks, as reflected by the lower M A E and P E values in datasets A, C, and E. Nevertheless, the performance of the ASMA-based model remains dataset-dependent, indicating that improvements are needed to ensure robust predictive accuracy across a broader range of operating conditions.

A purely data-driven RF model is also constructed. The model uses the process inputs (i.e., conductivity κ , concentration of modulator c 1 , and initial conditions) to directly predict the outlet mAb concentration, without relying on any mechanistic prior. Calibration results and validation performance of the purely data-driven model are reported in the Supplementary Material (Section S.2) . The purely data-driven model, while achieving moderate accuracy for datasets B and D, underperforms in complex conditions like those in datasets A, C, and E, where the lack of mechanistic priors prevents it from generalizing well to nonlinear retention behaviour ( Table 9 ).

#### Construction of the hybrid model

We then proceed with the construction of hybrid models following the methodology described in Section 2.1 . Two hybrid configurations are developed, each built upon a different mechanistic component, i.e., one based on the LKM coupled with the Langmuir adsorption isotherm, and the other on the LKM coupled with the ASMA isotherm. For both configurations, RF regressors are constructed on four of the five available experimental datasets, with a leave-one-out cross-validation scheme to ensure robust model assessment. The inputs to the RF consist of the outlet concentration and the internal states predicted by the mechanistic component (i.e., c m A b and q m A b ), together with the conductivity profile κ . RF hyperparameters are optimised via five-fold cross-validation, and a forest size of 100 trees is selected in both cases.

For sake of conciseness, only cross-validation results are shown in Figs. 7 and 8 , which illustrate the predictive performance of the hybrid models across the five experimental datasets built on the LKM coupled with Langmuir adsorption isotherm and the ASMA isotherm, respectively. It is important to state that both hybrid and purely data-driven ( Section 4.2.1 ) configurations exhibit excellent performance in calibration, consistently achieving low error values across all datasets.

Metrics used for the assessment of the predictive capabilities of the constructed models both in calibration and validation (i.e., M A E and P E values) are reported in Table 10 . The hybrid model built on the Langmuir isotherm performs reasonably well only for datasets B and D, where system behaviour appears simpler and closer to the assumptions of the Langmuir model. Instead, it fails to correct the poor predictions of the mechanistic model for datasets A, C, and E, where peak broadening and delayed elution dominate the chromatographic response, as confirmed by high P E values.

In contrast, the hybrid model based on the ASMA isotherm achieves significantly better accuracy across most scenarios, with notable improvements in datasets A, C, and D. For example, in dataset A, the M A E drops from 1.22 g L −1 (mechanistic) and 1.19 g L −1 (purely data-driven) to 0.28 g L −1 , which corresponds to a reduction of approximately 77 % and 76 % , respectively. Large improvement is also evident in dataset C, where the hybrid-ASMA model reaches a M A E of 0.57 g L −1 , reducing the M A E by 69 % and 41 % compared to the purely data-driven and mechanistic models, respectively.

Although it still exhibits limitations in dataset E (cr. Tables 9 and 10 ), the model succeeds in capturing the shape and timing of the elution peak more effectively, indicating that the more flexible ASMA structure allows for better integration with the data-driven correction.

Key outcomes from this analysis can be summarised as follows: • The hybrid model based on the ASMA isotherm shows the best overall predictive performance across the majority of validation cases, with up to 77 % reduction in M A E compared to both the purely data-driven and the mechanistic models (dataset A). The only exception is dataset E, where the MAE is higher when using the hybrid model. It should be noted, however, that the peak time and shape prediction is improved. In scenarios characterized by more complex elution behaviours (e.g., datasets A and C), peak predictions are improved in both magnitude and timing; • The hybrid model based on the Langmuir adsorption isotherm performs well only in simple scenarios (i.e., dataset B and D), while fails in presence of delayed elution peaks. The trend is also reflected by the purely data-driven model, that shows competitive performance only when datasets B and D are used for validation; • With specific concern on dataset E, the purely mechanistic ASMA model outperforms both the hybrid and data-driven alternatives, indicating that hybridisation may not always be beneficial, e.g., when the mechanistic component already captures the essential dynamics and in the presence of limited calibration datasets.

### Discussion

The results confirm the potential of hybrid modelling strategies to enhance the predictive performance of standalone mechanistic and data-driven models, based on the level of mechanistic knowledge and the amount of information available for model training. However, the benefit of hybridisation is not unconditional and depends on several interacting factors. Regarding the impact of the two factors analyzed on hybrid model performance, namely (i) mechanistic model accuracy and (ii) amount of information available for model calibration, the following observations can be drawn: (i) Hybrid modelling is not always advantageous. The benefits depend on the specific characteristics of the system under study and the available information. The accuracy of the mechanistic component plays a pivotal role in shaping the hybrid performance: when it provides a good approximation of the process (as observed for mechanistic model #1, the ASMA-based chromatography model, and the urethane case study) hybrid structure significantly improves prediction accuracy and generalisation, even under extrapolative conditions. However, when the mechanistic component is too simplistic or misleading (as for mechanistic model #3 or Langmuir-based chromatography model under salt-varying conditions), and the data are insufficient to compensate, the added complexity of hybridisation cannot be justified. (ii) The amount of information available for model training critically influences the predictive capability of the hybrid model. Our analysis shows that hybrid models maintain good predictive performance even in low-data regimes, provided that the mechanistic component is sufficiently informative. In contrast, purely data-driven models are highly sensitive to the amount of data available for the calibration and may fail to extrapolate. However, when both data and mechanistic insights are scarce, hybrid models struggle as well. Even, results from the chromatography case (dataset E) show that in some scenarios, the standalone mechanistic model can outperform the hybrid one. Finally, the urethane case study (Supplementary material) shows consistent hybrid model performance, even when the noise in the data increases.

## Conclusions

In this work, we proposed a methodology to develop hybrid model configurations, focusing on the interplay between mechanistic model fidelity and the amount of information available for model training. A central objective was to assess the conditions under which a hybrid approach offers real advantages over purely mechanistic or data-driven alternatives. Results suggest that the effectiveness of hybrid modelling critically depends on both the quality of the mechanistic component and the availability of data. When mechanistic knowledge offers a reliable process representation, hybrid models can outperform purely data-driven alternatives, particularly in data-scarce settings. However, when the mechanistic component is overly simplistic or misleading, and data are insufficient to compensate, the added complexity of hybridisation may not yield benefits.

Future work could explore the application of the methodology to other case studies, including processes from different industries, in order to assess its generalizability and to evaluate how hybrid modelling strategies perform under diverse process and data availability conditions.
